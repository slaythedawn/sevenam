## L1 — Mon 31 Aug, 07:13 — the model

> In two years you'll either own an ad account that's worth something, or you'll have been
> renting one.
>
> That gets decided in the first week of an engagement, and almost nobody reads closely
> enough at the time to notice.
>
> Here's what ownership actually means, item by item. The Business Manager is in your
> company's name. The ad account sits inside it. The pixel is yours and has been collecting
> against your domain since day one. The audiences built off that pixel are yours. The
> creative files too, the working files and the raw footage, not just the exported videos.
>
> If all of that is true, changing who runs your advertising is an administrative task. You
> remove one user and add another. Nothing is lost.
>
> If it isn't true, leaving costs you the account history, the learning, and the audiences
> you paid to build. You start from zero with whoever comes next. Which means the renewal
> conversation was never really about the work.
>
> Two things worth doing this week, and neither requires you to change anything or upset
> anyone.
>
> Open Business Manager and check whose name is on it. Not who has access. Who owns it.
>
> Then ask where the raw creative files live. If the answer is a folder nobody can quite
> name, that's your answer.
>
> You only need to do it once.

## L2 — Tue 1 Sep, 07:34 — creative

> The ceiling on most accounts isn't targeting or budget. It's how many genuinely different
> ideas you can put in front of the platform each week.
>
> Automation moved that constraint and most teams haven't restructured around it yet.
> Audience discovery, placement, budget distribution across creative, all handled now, at no
> extra cost. What's left is supplying something worth choosing between, and deciding
> quickly about what comes back.
>
> So the useful question isn't whether your ads are good. It's whether you could retire a
> fatiguing ad tomorrow without leaving a hole.
>
> If you couldn't, you aren't producing enough, and every other optimisation is decorating a
> bottleneck.
>
> What enough looks like in practice is several genuinely different concepts a week. Not
> variations on one image. A different angle, a different opening three seconds, a different
> format. Variations produce effects too small to detect at the volume most accounts run,
> which is why so much testing concludes nothing and everyone agrees to look again next
> month.
>
> If you can't change who makes the work, you can still change what you ask for. Brief for
> concepts instead of executions. Ask for three different reasons to care, not three
> headlines for the same one.
>
> And keep every loser documented. The pattern in them is most of what makes the next batch
> better, and it's the part everyone throws away.

## L3 — Wed 2 Sep, 12:41 — the model

> The fee you're quoted isn't the fee you pay, and the real number takes about two minutes to
> work out.
>
> Add the management fee. Add creative production, which is quoted separately in most
> arrangements. Add any platform, reporting or tool fees sitting alongside it. Divide the
> total by your media spend.
>
> That's your effective percentage. It's the only number worth comparing between two
> providers, and it's almost always higher than the headline.
>
> For context on what you're comparing against. Most Australian agencies price Meta
> management as a percentage of ad spend, commonly ten to twenty per cent of monthly media,
> usually with a minimum monthly fee underneath it. Flat retainers for small and mid sized
> businesses commonly run from around eight hundred dollars to five thousand and beyond,
> depending on scope. Hybrid deals sit in between, a lower base plus ten to thirty per cent
> of verified results above an agreed baseline.
>
> Those are market observations, not our rates.
>
> Two questions worth running alongside the arithmetic.
>
> What happens to this fee at double my current spend. If the answer is that it doubles,
> you're paying twice for growth you funded yourself, once in media and once in fee, for
> work that didn't double.
>
> And is creative inside or outside this number. Creative volume is the single biggest driver
> of performance in most accounts, so a management fee that excludes it is quoting you for
> half the job.
>
> Neither question commits you to anything. They're just the two that rarely get asked before
> signing.

## L4 — Thu 3 Sep, 07:09 — craft

> Most accounts get judged on the wrong number, and the wrong number always looks better.
>
> In platform ROAS counts the conversions the platform believes it caused. Blended revenue
> against total spend counts what actually arrived in the business. When those two disagree,
> the second one is the one your bank account agrees with.
>
> Three habits fix most of it, and none of them need a new agency or a new tool.
>
> Judge on cost per result rather than click through rate. A high click through rate on a bad
> ad means you've written something people want to click and not something they want to buy.
> It flatters bad work reliably.
>
> Let a test leave the learning phase before you conclude anything. Judging in the first
> couple of days is the most common and most expensive mistake there is, and it's usually
> made because somebody asked on Tuesday how it was going.
>
> Change one meaningful thing at a time, or you can't attribute the result to anything and
> you've spent the budget learning nothing.
>
> Then watch frequency as the early warning. When it climbs, the creative is the problem. Not
> the audience, not the bid, not the platform quietly getting worse.
>
> All of that is a decision about what goes in the report and what you refuse to conclude
> from it. Which is yours, whoever is running the account.

## L5 — Fri 4 Sep, 07:28 — creative

> A brief that asks for three headlines will get you three headlines. It's the wrong ask, and
> it's the most common one.
>
> The differences that move an account are conceptual. A different reason to care. A
> different opening three seconds. A different format entirely. Not a different background
> and not a different button.
>
> So brief for concepts, and let the brief carry the thinking rather than the execution.
>
> What a good one contains. The specific person who should recognise themselves in the first
> second, because a generic opening gets skipped by everyone equally. The one thing that has
> to land if nothing else does. The objection this particular ad exists to handle. What you
> already know doesn't work, taken from the losers you documented. And the real format
> constraints, which are usually vertical, sound off, and three seconds to stop a thumb.
>
> Then ask for three different answers to that. Not three versions of one answer.
>
> The test of a brief is whether two people could read it and come back with genuinely
> different work. If they'd come back with the same idea wearing different clothes, the brief
> already made the decisions that should have been theirs.
>
> This one sits entirely within your control, whoever ends up making the work.

## L6 — Sat 5 Sep, 09:54 — craft

> If somebody bought your business tomorrow, the ad account is one of the first things they'd
> open. Here's what they'd actually look at.
>
> Not the ROAS. They'll rebuild that themselves from your P&L.
>
> They'd check whose Business Manager it is, because they're buying an acquisition engine and
> they need to know it comes with the company. An account sitting inside a provider's
> Business Manager doesn't transfer cleanly, and neither do the audiences and the learning
> built up inside it.
>
> They'd check the pixel has been firing against your domain consistently, and for how long.
> Historical data is the one part nobody can recreate after the fact.
>
> They'd check whether the conversion events map to money rather than to page views, because
> every decision made on top of badly defined events was made on data that was quietly wrong.
>
> They'd look at creative volume over the last twelve months, because it tells them whether
> the account was being run or just maintained.
>
> And they'd want the raw creative files. Not the exports.
>
> The reason to look at this now rather than later is that getting a business ready to sell
> reportedly takes eighteen to twenty four months, and the ownership questions are the ones
> you can't retrofit. You can improve a margin inside a quarter. You can't go back and own
> three years of pixel history you didn't own at the time.
>
> Worth an hour whether or not you're selling anything.

## L7 — Sun 6 Sep, 10:16 — the model

> If you're spending under three thousand a month on Meta, don't hire an agency. Run it
> yourself.
>
> That's on our own site and I say it on calls, and the arithmetic is the reason.
>
> Most agencies price as a percentage of ad spend, commonly ten to twenty per cent of monthly
> media, with a minimum monthly fee underneath it, often five hundred to a thousand dollars
> and up. The minimum exists so small accounts don't fall below what the agency will service.
>
> At three thousand a month the percentage barely matters. The minimum is what you're
> actually paying. Five hundred is seventeen per cent of your media. A thousand is thirty
> three.
>
> You're not buying expertise at that point. You're buying somebody's floor.
>
> It changes further up. Somewhere past ten thousand a month, when the account needs a
> decision every day and nobody has time to make it, paying someone starts to make sense.
>
> Under three, here's the whole method instead.
>
> Own the Business Manager yourself. Install the pixel and the conversions API and verify
> both actually fire. Define events that map to money rather than page views. Few campaigns,
> broad targeting, let the platform find people. Ship new creative every week, different
> concepts rather than colour variants. Give changes time to leave the learning phase. Judge
> on blended revenue against total spend. Change one thing at a time.
>
> That's it. It isn't secret and it isn't complicated.
>
> What's hard is doing it every single day when you've also got a business to run. That's the
> actual thing anyone is selling you, and it's worth knowing that's what you're buying.
