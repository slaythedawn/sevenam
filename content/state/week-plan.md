## L1 — Mon 31 Aug, 07:12 — the model

> If you're spending under three thousand a month on Meta, don't hire an agency.
>
> Run it yourself. I say that on calls and it's written on our own site.
>
> Here's the arithmetic nobody walks you through.
>
> Most Australian agencies price Meta management as a percentage of ad spend, commonly ten
> to twenty per cent of monthly media. Underneath that sits a minimum monthly fee, often
> five hundred to a thousand dollars and up. The minimum exists so small accounts don't
> fall below what the agency will service.
>
> At three thousand a month in media, the percentage barely matters. The minimum is what
> you're actually paying. Five hundred is seventeen per cent of your media. A thousand is
> thirty-three.
>
> You're not buying expertise at that point. You're buying someone's floor.
>
> The shape of it changes further up. Somewhere past ten thousand a month, when the account
> needs a decision every day and nobody has the time to make it, paying someone starts to
> make sense. Under three, it doesn't, and anyone telling you otherwise is quoting you
> their minimum.
>
> I'd rather say that now than take the retainer.

## L2 — Tue 1 Sep, 07:23 — craft

> Every ad your competitors are running right now is public, and almost nobody reads them
> properly.
>
> The usual mistake is treating a competitor's ad as proof that something works. You can't
> see that. For ordinary commercial ads the Library shows what's running and when it
> started. No spend, no results, no audience. Those get published only for political and
> social issue advertising.
>
> So a competitor's ad is evidence of a decision. It isn't evidence of a result.
>
> There is one signal in there that's close to real, and it's duration. An ad that's been
> running continuously for months is almost certainly earning its place, because advertisers
> switch off what loses money. An ad that appeared last week tells you nothing.
>
> So sort your attention by how long something has been live. Then look at what the
> long-runners have structurally in common. The hook. The format. The length. What actually
> happens in the first three seconds.
>
> Then build your own version of the mechanism. Don't copy the execution. A close imitation
> puts you second in someone else's test, with less credibility and no idea which part did
> the work.
>
> One more thing while you're in there. Your library is public too. Worth remembering
> before you put an aggressive offer inside an ad rather than behind a click.

## L3 — Wed 2 Sep, 12:38 — the model

> The fee your agency quotes you isn't the fee you pay.
>
> Here's how to work out the real one. It takes about two minutes.
>
> Add the management fee. Add what creative costs you, which is quoted separately in most
> arrangements. Add any platform, reporting or tool fees sitting alongside it. Then divide
> the total by your media spend.
>
> That's your effective percentage. It's the only number worth comparing between two
> providers, and it is almost always higher than the headline.
>
> The reason it matters is that a management fee excluding creative is paying for half the
> job. Creative volume is the single biggest driver of performance in most accounts, so a
> quote that leaves it out isn't cheaper. It's incomplete.
>
> Two questions worth asking before you sign anything.
>
> What happens to this fee at double my current spend. If the answer is that it doubles,
> you're funding your own growth twice, once in media and once in fee, for work that didn't
> double.
>
> And what do I own at the end. If the account, the pixel and the creative files live in
> the provider's Business Manager, leaving costs you the asset. An arrangement where they
> own it has to be renewed. One where you own it has to be earned.

Image: **card D**. The effective-percentage arithmetic as rows.

## L4 — Thu 3 Sep, 07:08 — ai-tools

> Advantage+ automates the part that used to be the job. It can't automate the part that
> matters.
>
> That's not a criticism of it. In accounts with clean conversion data and a decent supply
> of creative it frequently beats a manually structured campaign, because the platform tests
> combinations faster than a person can.
>
> Audience discovery, placement, budget distribution across creative. All handled, at no
> extra cost. A large share of what agencies historically charged for.
>
> What it can't do is make your creative. It can't fix tracking that's reporting the wrong
> events. It can't decide what a customer is worth to you. And it won't tell you when a
> winning ad has quietly gone stale in a way the in-platform numbers flatter.
>
> So the honest way to describe it isn't that automation replaced the work. It raised the
> floor and moved the constraint.
>
> Which means the accounts that win now are the ones supplying more and better creative,
> and making faster decisions about what to feed it.
>
> Automation amplifies whatever you give it. That cuts both ways, and most accounts I read
> find that out the expensive way.

## L5 — Fri 4 Sep, 07:31 — creative

> Most creative testing concludes nothing, and the reason is that it's testing the wrong
> thing.
>
> What usually gets called a test is variation testing. Same idea, different headline.
> Different background. Different button. Those produce small effects that need enormous
> volume to detect, and most accounts don't have that volume. So the test runs, the numbers
> come back inside the noise, and everyone agrees to try again next month.
>
> The differences that actually move an account are conceptual. A different reason to care.
> A different opening three seconds. A different format entirely.
>
> So test angles against each other. Only refine within an angle once one is clearly
> winning.
>
> A few things that follow from that, and they're the ones I'd argue for hardest.
>
> Ship new concepts weekly rather than in one monthly batch. Let a test leave the learning
> phase before you draw a conclusion, because judging in the first couple of days is the
> most common and most expensive mistake there is. Judge on cost per result, not
> click-through rate, which flatters bad ads reliably.
>
> And keep every loser documented. Knowing which angles fail is most of what makes the next
> batch better, and it's the part everyone throws away.
>
> The test of whether you're producing enough: can you retire a fatiguing ad without
> leaving a hole. If you can't, you aren't.

## L7 — Sun 6 Sep, 10:11 — the model

> An arrangement where the provider owns the asset has to be renewed. One where you own it
> has to be earned.
>
> That single sentence is most of what I think about how this should work.
>
> When the ad account, the pixel, the audiences and the creative files sit inside an
> agency's Business Manager, the relationship has a lock on it. Leaving doesn't just mean
> finding someone new. It means losing the account history, the learning, the audiences you
> paid to build. So the renewal conversation isn't really about the work. It's about the
> cost of moving.
>
> Flip the ownership and everything downstream changes. If it's all in your Business Manager
> from the first hour, there's nothing to negotiate over if it ends. You keep the asset
> either way.
>
> Which means the only thing keeping the arrangement alive is whether it's worth it this
> month.
>
> That's a harder way to run a business and I think it's the correct one. It also happens
> to be the only version I'd have signed, back when I was the one signing.
